It's one of the most searched questions in the lottery space: what Pick 3 number comes in the most?

Most sites answer it with an all-time frequency list — digit 5 has appeared 4,312 times, digit 7 has appeared 4,287 times, and so on. These lists look authoritative. They're also almost entirely useless for making picks today.

Here's why — and what actually tells you something worth acting on.

---

## Why "Most Common Overall" Is the Wrong Question

Pick 3 is not a one-position game. It's a three-position game. When you play a number like 4-7-2, you're making three separate positional bets:

- Digit 4 in position 1
- Digit 7 in position 2
- Digit 2 in position 3

An all-time frequency list treats all three positions as one pool. It tells you that digit 7 has appeared 4,287 times across all positions combined. But it tells you nothing about whether digit 7 belongs in position 1, 2, or 3 tonight.

That's the critical gap. Digit 7 could be appearing almost exclusively in position 3 recently while being cold in positions 1 and 2. Playing it in position 1 based on its overall frequency ranking would be based on irrelevant data.

**Position-specific frequency is the only frequency data that matters for Pick 3.**

---

## Why Time Window Matters Just as Much

Even position-specific frequency data goes stale. A digit that was hot in position 2 three months ago may be cold there now. Patterns shift over time — not because lottery machines develop preferences, but because random distributions produce natural clustering and then disperse.

A 45-year all-time frequency table is statistically smooth — over tens of thousands of draws, every digit approaches exactly 10% frequency in every position, as probability theory predicts. There's almost no useful signal in that data for tonight's draw.

What does carry signal is recent data — the last 7 to 60 draws. This is where genuine patterns emerge and where hot digits can be identified with some statistical meaning.

---

## What Position-Specific Frequency Actually Shows

Here's how position-specific frequency analysis works in practice.

In any analysis window, each digit has an expected frequency of exactly 10% per position. In a 30-draw window, that's 3.0 expected appearances per digit per position.

Digits running above that rate are hot in that position. Digits running below it are cold. The further from expected, the stronger the signal.

For example, over a 30-draw window:
- Digit 5 appearing 6 times in position 1 = running at 2× expected = genuinely hot
- Digit 3 appearing 1 time in position 2 = running at 0.33× expected = cold
- Digit 8 appearing 3 times in position 3 = exactly on expectation = neutral

These three readings give you something actionable: a strong candidate for position 1, a digit to avoid in position 2, and a neutral in position 3.

---

## How Skip Tracking Adds a Second Layer

Frequency alone doesn't tell the complete story. Skip tracking adds a second dimension — how many draws have passed since a digit last appeared in a specific position.

A digit can have moderate overall frequency but a high current skip — meaning it appeared several times early in the window but hasn't been seen recently. That pattern looks different from a digit with the same total count but a recent appearance.

The expected skip interval for any digit in any position is approximately 10 draws. A digit absent for 15+ draws in a specific position is statistically overdue — what analysts call "late." Absent for 20+ draws is "very late."

When a digit is both running hot in recent frequency AND has a lower-than-average current skip, that's a convergence of signals worth noting. When a digit is cold in frequency AND has a high skip, it may be setting up for a rebalancing run.

Neither signal is a prediction. Both signals are data points worth factoring into your selection.
